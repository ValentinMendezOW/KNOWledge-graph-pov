# Implementing Generative AI with Speed and Safety

**Source:** McKinsey & Company (Risk & Resilience Practice)
**Authors:** Oliver Bevan et al.
**Published:** March 13, 2024
**Original URL:** https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/risk-and-resilience/our-insights/implementing-generative-ai-with-speed-and-safety

---

## Overview

This McKinsey article addresses a critical tension in enterprise AI adoption: how to move quickly to capture generative AI's enormous economic potential while managing the significant and evolving risks the technology introduces. Based on a survey of over 100 entities with $50 million or more in annual revenue, the report reveals a stark preparedness gap and provides a four-step framework for organizations to implement gen AI responsibly and at pace.

## Key Statistics

- **Gen AI could contribute up to $4.4 trillion** in economic value globally
- The technology can **enhance overall AI impact by 15–40%**
- **63% of surveyed organizations** rank gen AI implementation as a "high" or "very high" priority
- **91% of those same organizations** report they do not feel "very prepared" to implement gen AI in a responsible manner
- This 63% vs. 91% gap — high ambition but low preparedness — is the central finding driving the article's recommendations

## Gen AI Capabilities

The report notes gen AI's ability to generate:

- Credible software code
- Text and speech content
- High-fidelity images
- Interactive videos
- Molecular models for pharmaceutical research
- Crystal structure identification for new materials discovery

## The Eight Risk Categories

McKinsey identifies gen-AI-related risks in **eight main categories**:

1. **Inaccuracy / Hallucinations** — Gen AI models produce outputs that are factually incorrect, fabricated, or misleading, yet presented with high confidence
2. **Impaired Fairness / Bias** — Output may be inherently biased against particular groups of users based on skin tone, gender, ethnicity, or other characteristics embedded in training data
3. **Intellectual Property Infringement** — Copyright violations, plagiarism, and unauthorized use of proprietary content in model training or outputs
4. **Privacy Violations** — Unauthorized public disclosure of personal or sensitive information; risks around data used in training and inference
5. **Cybersecurity Risks** — New attack vectors including prompt injection, data poisoning, model theft, and adversarial exploitation of AI systems
6. **Malicious Use** — Dissemination of false content, deepfakes, large-scale misinformation campaigns, and malicious influence on politics and personal well-being
7. **Data Privacy and Quality** — Issues with the quality, provenance, and governance of data used to train and fine-tune models
8. **Performance and Explainability** — Lack of transparency in how models arrive at outputs; difficulty auditing or explaining AI-driven decisions

### Risk Prevalence (from McKinsey's 2024 Global Survey)

Among organizations that have experienced negative consequences from gen AI:

- **Inaccuracy** is the most commonly reported risk affecting organizations
- **Cybersecurity** is the second most commonly reported
- **Explainability** ranks third
- **Intellectual property** and **fairness** concerns are also significant but less frequently reported as having already caused harm

## The Impact of Missteps

McKinsey warns that missteps and failures can:

- **Erode the confidence** of executives, employees, and customers
- **Trigger scaling back** to "ultrasafe" use cases that generate limited risk but also fail to capitalize on the technology's true potential
- Create a vicious cycle where fear of risk leads to under-investment, which leads to falling behind competitors

## Three Organizational Archetypes

McKinsey identifies three implementation archetypes based on how organizations engage with gen AI:

1. **Makers** — Organizations that develop their own foundation models from scratch (highest investment, highest customization, highest risk exposure)
2. **Shapers** — Organizations that customize and scale existing foundation models for their specific needs (moderate investment, significant customization)
3. **Takers** — Organizations that adopt off-the-shelf gen AI applications with minimal customization (lowest investment, fastest deployment, narrower risk profile)

Each archetype faces a different risk profile and requires tailored governance approaches.

## Four-Step Risk Management Framework

McKinsey presents a structured framework for organizations to manage gen AI risks while maintaining implementation speed:

### Step 1: Rapid Assessment Sprint

- Execute a rapid assessment sprint to understand inbound gen AI risk exposure
- Map the organization's current and planned gen AI use cases
- Identify which of the eight risk categories apply to each use case
- Prioritize based on materiality and likelihood

### Step 2: Comprehensive Materiality Assessment

- Build a comprehensive materiality assessment across all domains and use cases
- Evaluate risks at the use-case level, not just at the technology level
- Develop both **technical mitigation options** (guardrails, filters, red-teaming) and **non-technical mitigation options** (policies, training, human-in-the-loop processes)
- Score use cases on a risk matrix (high/medium/low) across all eight categories

### Step 3: Establish Governance Structure

- Establish governance that balances expertise, oversight, and rapid decision-making capability
- Adapt existing organizational risk structures rather than building entirely new ones
- Ensure governance enables speed — overly bureaucratic processes will slow adoption and cede competitive advantage
- Include technical experts, legal/compliance, business stakeholders, and ethics perspectives

### Step 4: Embed Governance in the Operating Model

- Embed governance within the operating model, not as an afterthought
- Leverage cross-functional expertise in day-to-day operations
- Invest in end-user training so that frontline users understand risks and guardrails
- Make governance a continuous process, not a one-time assessment — risks evolve as models and use cases change

## Key Recommendations

1. **Move responsibly AND with good pace** — Organizations can "move responsibly and with good pace to capture the value" by adapting established risk management practices to address gen AI's unique challenges
2. **Don't let fear paralyze action** — The risk of inaction (falling behind competitors) is as real as the risk of moving too fast
3. **Invest in ongoing risk management** — Organizations will need "additional, ongoing efforts to understand and manage the risks of the technology's adoption, which will likely require an investment of time and resources and a shift in ways of working"
4. **Tailor approach to archetype** — Makers, Shapers, and Takers each need different governance approaches calibrated to their risk exposure
5. **Build cross-functional governance** — Effective gen AI governance requires coordination across technology, legal, compliance, ethics, and business functions

## Broader Context: Industry Risk Management Trends

From McKinsey's 2024 global AI survey, organizations are responding to gen AI risks through:

- **Manual review processes** for reliability (addressing inaccuracy)
- **Enhanced cybersecurity safeguards** around AI systems and data handling
- **IP protection measures** to avoid unintentional violations
- **Compliance and ethics emphasis** on transparency, fairness, and legal alignment
- **Dedicated AI governance structures** (Centers of Excellence, AI leadership councils)
- **Redesigned workflows** embedding AI directly into processes with built-in guardrails
- **Executive engagement** in AI strategy at the C-suite level
- **Broad upskilling programs** with role-based employee training

## Relevant Standards Referenced

- **ISO/IEC 42001** — First global AI management system standard providing structured approach for establishing and maintaining AI systems with emphasis on transparency and traceability
- **NIST AI Risk Management Framework** — Flexible, risk-based framework for identifying, assessing, and managing AI risks across development and deployment

## Conclusion

The article concludes that the gap between gen AI ambition (63% high priority) and preparedness (91% not very prepared) represents both an urgent challenge and an opportunity. Organizations that can close this gap — moving with both speed and safety — will capture disproportionate value. The key is not to choose between speed and safety but to build frameworks that enable both simultaneously through structured risk assessment, tailored governance, and continuous adaptation.
